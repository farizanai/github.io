Fewer parameters, fewer joules? Inside ReSPLIN

November 2026 · LLM compression · energy-efficient inference · ~7 min read

Most LLM pruning papers report how many parameters or FLOPs they removed. I wanted to know something more practical: when you prune a model, does the GPU actually burn less energy? Surprisingly often, the answer is no.

Unstructured sparsity leaves zeros inside matrices the GPU still multiplies at full size. Even structured methods can produce awkward operator shapes, extra kernels, or extra memory traffic. GPU energy depends on all of those, not just the parameter count. ReSPLIN (Region-Wise Structured Pruning and Low-Rank Factorization for LLM INference) is my attempt at a post-training, calibration-only compression scheme whose savings show up on a power meter, with no retraining or fine-tuning.

1 · Not all layers are equally compressible

I started by asking which layers can absorb damage. Two causal probes on LLaMA 3.2 3B gave a clear picture. Perturbing previous-token states in the first layers shifts the final output distribution about 4.1× more than the interior average, because those layers route context. Attenuating the last layers' updates shifts it about 9.2× more, because they read out the prediction. The middle of the network recovers from perturbations much better.

So ReSPLIN splits the decoder into three regions. Root (the first two and last two layers) stays dense. Stem (the first two-thirds of the interior) gets structured MLP channel pruning. Cap (the remaining third) gets light low-rank factorization of its attention Q and O projections.

Does the split matter? On LLaMA 3.2 1B, I held the parameter budget at about 15% and moved the region boundaries around. Removing the protected Root more than tripled perplexity. Protecting only the first two layers helped a lot, and protecting only the last two did not. Swapping which region gets which treatment gave a tiny bit more energy saving but much worse quality.

2 · Prune whole channels, physically

In the Stem, every MLP channel gets a saliency score that combines its calibration activations with the strength of its downstream weights. The pruning budget is then handed out greedily: at each step, the layer that loses the least saliency per extra 10% of pruning gets pruned a bit more, with a penalty that protects layers near the boundaries. The surviving channels are kept consistently across the gate, up and down projections, and the width is rounded to a multiple of 64. The MLP really gets smaller. There are no masks and no zeros, so the GPU runs smaller dense matrix multiplies.

In the Cap, CapSVD factorizes a few Q/O projections with an activation-weighted SVD. Its rank rule refuses any factorization that would cost more than the dense matrix. That rule gets its own post.

3 · What the power meter says

I measured full-model prefill energy on an NVIDIA RTX 5000 Ada. NVML power was sampled at 100 Hz and integrated over at least 3 s, idle power was subtracted, and the result was normalised per token. Every method ran under identical settings. Pick a context length below. A point to the right saves energy, and a point lower loses less perplexity.

Only two methods save energy and raise throughput at every context length from 512 to 16K tokens: ReSPLIN and SliceGPT. SliceGPT pays about 3–4× more perplexity for it (ΔPPL 17–24 against ReSPLIN's 4.7–5.7). Among the methods that change the model's structure, ReSPLIN has the smallest perplexity cost.

4 · Does it hold up on other models and tasks?

At the default operating point (30% Stem pruning plus a 20% CapSVD target, about 10% of parameters removed), I evaluated three models on two long-context QA sets and two language-modelling sets. Every one of the 24 configurations saves energy and runs faster.

Honest caveats. The 1B model is fragile: QA-F1 drops by 6–12 points on NarrativeQA, while the 3B models lose only about 0.7–2.8 points. My ablations also show that Stem MLP pruning delivers nearly all the energy saving. CapSVD on its own reduces parameters but not energy, so ReSPLIN uses it only as a light secondary step.

Takeaway

Parameter count is a proxy, and an unreliable one. If the goal is energy, compression has to produce smaller dense operators, aligned to the hardware, and it has to leave alone the layers the network can't recover from. ReSPLIN is a simple, static, retraining-free way to do that.
