Can an LLM out-optimize Vivado? Rewriting RTL for FPGA power, performance and area

November 2026 · LLMs for hardware design · FPGA · ~7 min read

FPGA synthesis tools are very good at optimizing the circuit your RTL describes. They are not allowed to change which circuit that is. I wanted to measure how much an LLM can gain by rewriting the RTL itself, on top of everything Vivado already does.

Vivado simplifies logic, removes redundant registers, retimes and replicates. Every one of those moves has to preserve the RTL's cycle-level behaviour, its latencies and its reset semantics. Reusing one adder across cycles, swapping a shift-and-add multiplier for a DSP slice, or moving a stack from flip-flops into LUTRAM all need a different RTL. Previous LLM work mostly asks "is the generated Verilog correct?". This work asks "is the rewritten Verilog cheaper, after the vendor tool has done its best on both versions?"

The setup: three roles, real Vivado reports

The LLM plays three separate roles. Each is its own request with its own prompt, and each sees only the previous role's output, not its reasoning. All feedback comes from real post-route Vivado reports on an AMD Alveo U200. Click a role to see what it does:

The comparison is controlled. Four variants of every design go through Vivado 2025.2 at the same clock: the original RTL with the default flow, the original with an aggressive performance-oriented flow, and the GPT-5.6 and Qwen3.8-27B rewrites with that same aggressive flow. Because the last three share a flow, the gap between "Vivado Optimized" and an LLM variant is purely the effect of the rewrite. Power comes from switching activity recorded under identical stimulus for every variant.

Result 1: the tool alone barely moves the needle

The first surprise is that the performance-oriented flow made things slightly worse on average, because it replicates logic to chase timing. The LLM rewrites improve every metric:

Qwen's bigger bars come with a caveat. It passed verification on only 8 of 14 designs, failing on div16, radix2div and all four industrial modules. On the same 8 designs, GPT-5.6 roughly matches it on LUTs (−33.3% vs. −32.7%) and delay (−6.3% vs. −5.7%). Qwen stays ahead on dynamic power and flip-flops.

Result 2: design by design

The averages hide a lot of variety. Pick a metric and compare each rewrite against Vivado Optimized, the same flow on the original RTL:

Most of the gains come from mapping logic onto the FPGA's dedicated resources, which the tool can't do without permission to change the RTL:

  • multpipe8: GPT-5.6 moved the multiply into a DSP slice. LUTs dropped from 86 to 43, FFs from 92 to 45, and dynamic power fell 39%.
  • LIFO: the stack moved into four LUTRAMs, giving 45% fewer LUTs and 68% fewer FFs, but 19% more delay.
  • div16: LUTs went from 755 to 422 and delay fell from 20.7 to 16.6 ns.
  • ibex_multdiv_fast (industrial): the aggressive flow alone made it bigger and slower. GPT-5.6's rewrite cut power by 29% and FFs by 41%.

Not every rewrite helps. In multi16, GPT-5.6 cut LUTs by 31% but raised dynamic power from 0.78 to 1.27 mW. In multbooth8, Qwen pushed the whole multiplier into a DSP slice and used more power. That is why the reviewer works from measured numbers, not the model's own estimates.

Result 3: two very different ideas of "optimized"

The RTLLM multi_16bit shift-and-add multiplier shows how differently the two models think. The original picks one bit of a each cycle and adds b shifted by a variable amount, i−1. That variable shifter accounts for most of the 93 LUTs.

GPT-5.6 kept the algorithm and its exact 17-cycle timing, and just removed the expensive part. Qwen replaced the algorithm entirely: it is far smaller and 2 cycles instead of 17, but not cycle-equivalent. It passes the testbench, yet it is only usable if the surrounding system tolerates the latency change. Whether that counts as "correct" depends on your interface contract. Formal equivalence checking is the next thing I want in this loop.

Where LLMs didn't help. On ibex_alu and axil2apb, GPT-5.6 made no meaningful structural change, because the RTL was already close to what Vivado maps well. The Ibex register file was returned unchanged. The known FPGA-friendly version drops the reset on the storage array, and that changes reset behaviour, which the flow forbids. The LLM correctly declined to do it.

Takeaways

  • LLM rewriting complements the tool rather than replacing it. Gains come from structural changes (DSP, LUTRAM, shared arithmetic, merged registers) that the tool isn't allowed to make.
  • Reliability vs. aggressiveness. The proprietary model was reliable on every design. The local, 4-bit open-weight model was bolder on small designs but failed on the large ones. A model that keeps design IP on-site is attractive, but only once that gap closes.
  • Measure, don't trust. Some rewrites regressed power or delay. Post-route measurement and multi-stage verification are not optional.
